Stars are born when clouds of gas and dust collapse under their own gravity. Material that still carries angular momentum cannot fall straight onto the young star, so it flattens into a rotating disk around it. These **protoplanetary disks** typically survive for a few million years, and this is the window in which planets have to form.

Inside the disk, microscopic dust grains collide and stick together. Step by step they grow into pebbles, boulders and kilometre-sized planetesimals, which can then build planetary embryos. Giant planets also need to collect gas from the disk before it disperses. This growth is far from straightforward: grains tend to drift towards the star and can shatter when they collide too fast. Understanding how nature gets past these obstacles is one of the big open questions in planet formation.

### From a cloud to a planetary system

The figure below follows the path from a cold molecular cloud to a finished planetary system. Each stage shows a simplified sketch next to a real observation of an object at that stage. First, a dense **core** inside the cloud collapses under its own gravity (stage 2, L1544). Within about 100,000 years a young star forms, still embedded in its natal envelope and launching jets (stage 3, HOPS 315). After about a million years the envelope is gone and a flat, dusty **protoplanetary disk** remains, where planets are being built (stage 4, IM Lup). Within about ten million years the gas has dispersed and what is left is a planetary system, like the four giant planets imaged around HR 8799 (stage 5). The times on the arrows are rough ages, and they vary from star to star.
