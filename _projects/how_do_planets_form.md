Stars are born when clouds of gas and dust collapse under their own gravity. Material that still carries angular momentum cannot fall straight onto the young star, so it flattens into a rotating disc around it. These **protoplanetary discs** typically survive for a few million years, and this is the window in which planets have to form.

Inside the disc, microscopic dust grains collide and stick together. Step by step they grow into pebbles, boulders and kilometre-sized planetesimals, which can then build planetary embryos. Giant planets also need to collect gas from the disc before it disperses. This growth is far from straightforward: grains tend to drift towards the star and can shatter when they collide too fast. Understanding how nature gets past these obstacles is one of the big open questions in planet formation.
