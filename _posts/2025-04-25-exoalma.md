### The Goals

> *The program has had three main objectives: 1) to search for embedded protoplanets by examining the substructures that they create in the disk’s structure and gas kinematics, 2) to constrain the physical properties of these disk, such as their velocity, temperature, and density structure, and 3) to identify large-scale signatures of instabilities, such as GI, VSI or MRI.*

Over the past decade, disk observations of the dust continuum at millimeter and NIR wavelengths at sufficiently high-angular resolution have shown that planet-forming disks are highly structured, exhibiting symmetric gaps and rings, asymmetric crescents and arcs, or spirals (Bae et al., 2023; Benisty et al., 2023). The dust distribution reflects the underlying gas dynamics, as it depends on the strength of the coupling of grains to the gas motion (St-number). So, are most dust continuum rings formed via the accumulation of pebbles in gas pressure bumps? Is this process the primary mechanism driving the formation of dust substructure? Alternative processes include ice-lines (e.g., Saito and Sirono, 2011; Zhang et al., 2015), dust coagulation fronts (e.g., Ohashi et al., 2021), or second-generation dust rings via planetesimal collisions (e.g., Jiang and Ormel, 2021; Testi et al., 2022). To further examine the processes shaping the widely observed multi-wavelength dust substructures, analyzing the gas kinematics of protoplanetary disks is the next essential step.

After the exciting discoveries and the potential of early gas kinematical studies of protoplanetary disks, the first ALMA Large Program focusing on the analysis of gas kinematics around planet-forming disks was accepted and observed in ALMA's cycle 8 2021/2022 (ID: 2021.1.01123.L, PI: R. Teague). The exoALMA campaign targeted 15 large and bright planet-forming disks, most of which transition disks, at unprecedented spectral (~28 m s<sup>−1</sup>) and angular resolution (~0.1″) ([Loomis et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc43a); [Teague et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc43b)), pushing ALMA's capabilities to its current limit. The observational setup targeted the disk continuum and the molecular lines <sup>12</sup>CO (J = 3−2), <sup>13</sup>CO (J = 3−2), and CS (J = 7−6) in ALMA Band 7 with ~10 h on source-integration time, making it some of the deepest disk observations to date.

As the program's name suggests, its primary goal was to search for the youngest still embedded exoplanets, via the distinct imprints they leave on the gas velocity structure of the disk. However, with these exquisite observations, it has also been possible to investigate the dynamic and physical structure of protoplanetary disks in great detail. Planet formation is not an isolated process, but presumably happens simultaneously with (magneto)hydrodynamical instabilities that affect the disk's evolution (Lesur et al., 2023). It is thus crucial to investigate these processes to obtain a comprehensive picture of the physical and dynamical structure of planet-forming disks, for which exoALMA provided the necessary data quality.

In summary, the program has had three main objectives: 1) to search for embedded protoplanets by examining the substructures that they create in the disk's structure and gas kinematics, 2) to constrain the physical properties of these disk, such as their velocity, temperature, and density structure, and 3) to identify large-scale signatures of instabilities, such as GI, VSI or MRI. In the following paragraphs, I will summarize the main findings from the first wave of 17 exoALMA papers, on all of which I am a co-author, concerning these objectives.

### Summary of exoALMA results

The gallery of the exoALMA disk sample with its <sup>12</sup>CO (3-2) peak intensity and 0.9 mm dust continuum maps are presented in the two figures below. [Teague et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc43b) provides a detailed overview of the project's design, scientific goals, and key findings. It is already essential to note that the exoALMA sample is biased towards the largest and brightest planet-forming disks, allowing for the observation of their molecular line emission at high resolution. Furthermore, these disks were chosen to have already well-characterized dust substructures, indicative of the embedded planets we aimed to detect.

#### Optimized methodology

First, I summarize the optimized methods we developed within the collaboration to handle the exoALMA dataset. To begin, the ALMA interferometric observations must be calibrated and imaged to produce continuum and molecular line data cubes for analysis. [Loomis et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc43a) presents these methodologies, which were specifically developed for the data quality of exoALMA. We paid particular attention to the alignment of different execution blocks and phase decoherence, to *not* introduce artificial features resembling kinematic deviations we aim to detect. The robustness of the observed non-Keplerian features was further ensured by an alternative technique from CLEAN employing regularized maximum likelihood imaging, which allows for super-resolution images ([Zawadzki et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc434)). We benchmarked the most widely used hydrodynamics and radiative transfer codes, demonstrating high agreement when forward-modeling and interpreting kinematic perturbations of modeled planet-disk interactions ([Bae et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc436)).

In [Izquierdo et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc439), we present an improved methodology to account for the complex gaseous structures of our targets through channel-map modeling of the molecular line emissions. Utilizing and further developing the `discminer` code-package (Izquierdo et al., 2021), we could accurately describe the disks' emission morphologies and identify variations in the properties of these lines across our sample. We paid particular focus to the exact extraction of line properties, developing a novel two-component fit to disentangle the front and back side emission in our line moment maps. To evaluate the robustness of our moment maps, which form the basis of most of the analysis, [Hilder et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc435) investigated the spatial correlations derived from our model's posterior distribution. Our findings indicated that parameter uncertainties are underestimated by an order of magnitude when applying a more realistic probabilistic noise model.

#### Physical properties

The second cluster of papers focuses on determining the disk's physical properties, both on a global and more localized scale, i.e., radial profiles. Also in [Izquierdo et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc439), we present the `discminer` best-fit parameters of the exoALMA disks, including Keplerian stellar masses, geometrical properties, and gas surface emission heights. While in the `discminer` framework, the latter is described via an exponentially tapered power-law, [Galloway-Sprietsma et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc437) investigated substructures in the emission heights and line intensities. The paper further presents the global 2D temperature structures of a subsample of disks and finds a positive correlation between the emission height, disk extents, and gas disk masses.

The measurement of gas disk masses and surface densities from observations is of central importance, as it influences the overall disk evolution and comprises the available mass budget for forming planets in disks. Several works in our collaboration have hence focused on its determination. [Rosotti et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc42e) presents a model for the aforementioned correlation between emission heights and gas surface densities ([Galloway-Sprietsma et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc437); Paneque-Carreño et al., 2025), finding a median CO depletion of factor ~ 20 with respect to the interstellar medium. [Yoshida et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc42f) was able to measure the gas surface density from pressure-broadened CO line wings in the face-on transition disk of RXJ1604.3-2130A (see also [J1604]({{ '/blog/2023/j1604/' | relative_url }})). Another exciting aspect of the exoALMA dataset was the determination of gas surface density through modeling the gas rotation curve (Lodato et al., 2023; [Martire et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1051/0004-6361/202348546); Veronesi et al., 2021). This technique offers a significant advantage, as it does not require conversion factors to relate the measured CO line emission to H<sub>2</sub> abundances. Therefore, it is considered a more reliable estimate of the true gas disk masses.

Fitting to the rotation curves provided through my work ([Stadler et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adb152)), [Longarini et al., 2025a](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc431) obtained dynamical gas disk mass estimates for 10 sources of the exoALMA sample, all of which appear gravitationally stable (Q<sub>toom</sub> > 1). In addition, we combined the dynamical disk masses with the disk mass estimated from optical thin dust continuum emission. We found an averaged dust-to-gas mass ratio of ~ 400, statistically in line with the standard value of 100. Finally, using ancillary N<sub>2</sub>H<sup>+</sup> (J = 3−2) observations, [Trapman et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc430) measured disk masses based on integrated line fluxes and thermochemical models for 19 disks. These emission-based gas masses agree well with the kinematically measured ones within a factor of three, although they are systematically lower than dynamical estimates by a factor of two.

#### Substructures in emission and kinematics

The last set of papers investigates and interprets the observed emission and kinematical substructures in the search for embedded protoplanets and signatures of large-scale instabilities. [Curone et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc438) presents the 0.9 mm dust continuum maps of our sample (see below figure), modeling and analyzing their substructures, asymmetries, and faint outer disk emission. The paper also provides the best-fit geometrical parameters of the continuum visibility fits. Most asymmetric continuum disks with inner dust cavities show higher mass accretion rates and NIR excess, suggesting a connection between outer disk dust substructures and inner disk properties. [Galloway-Sprietsma et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc437) further reports the presence of substructures in the emission surfaces and radial intensity profiles in nearly all of the disks. Within each disk, these substructures often align between molecular tracers, intensity profiles, and kinematic perturbations.

Investigating the exoALMA channel maps in the search for localized kinematic substructures has probably been one of the most anticipated papers of the collaboration. [Pinte et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc433) highlights these velocity kinks, spiral arms, and filament-like features observed. We uncover the signatures of embedded planets in five disks (AA Tau, J1615, J1842, LkCa15 & SY Cha), with modeled masses ranging from 1 to 5 M<sub>Jup</sub> at orbital distances between 80-330 au. In the study of non-planetary driven features, [Barraza-Alfaro et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc42d) investigates large-scale turbulence within the disks observed by exoALMA. While the vertical shear instability (VSI) was not detected, some of the spiral-like velocity patterns observed could potentially be explained by the magneto-rotational instability (MRI) or gravitational instability (GI), although most of our disks have been determined to have gravitationally stable disk masses. On the other hand, some of the smooth, dynamically non-perturbed disks appear almost laminar. Finally, in exoALMA XVII., [Wölfer et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/adc42c) characterized the gas kinematics around dust asymmetries in the sample. The paper qualitatively compares the observed features to analytical vortex models and hydrodynamic simulations of the Rossby Wave instability (RWI) and dead zones. While the observations cannot provide evidence for the presence of an anticyclonic vortex for most disks, the disk around HD 135344B shows the most resemblance.
